The quick tour

  1. Start with the leaderboard. It ranks the six models and the IESO's official forecast by how far off they have been, and shows what each one predicts for the current hour and the next one, all in megawatts (MW). The chart below it shows every forecast against what really happened over the last 24 hours.
  2. Open a model. Every model page looks the same: this-hour and next-hour predictions, a 7-day forecast, a graph of past predictions against what happened, and an explorer. Use the Models menu at the top to switch between them.
  3. Click anywhere on a 7-day forecast line. That picks an hour. The explorer at the bottom of the page then shows what pushes demand up or down at that hour for that model.
  4. Try the sliders. Make it 10° warmer, cut the cloud cover, or turn the day into a holiday, and watch the prediction change instantly. Polynomial models also show their full written-out equation.
  5. Visit Comparison to see which model is most accurate, and how they all stack up against the IESO's own forecast.

Reading the cards

This hour / Next hour
Predictions for the hour on the clock now and the one after it. Times are Toronto time and each hour is labelled by when it starts, so "15:00" means 3:00 to 4:00 pm. The small grey text says which model made the number.
Actual demand this hour (so far)
The average of the grid operator's 5-minute readings since the hour began, so it is a like-for-like comparison with an hourly prediction. Early in the hour it is based on only a few readings and can still move. The percentage shows how far the prediction is from it.
Last completed hour
Actual demand for the hour that just ended. It is the average of the IESO's 5-minute readings for that hour, which match its official hourly file (published about a day later) to within 1 MW; the site switches to the official number once it appears. In the first few minutes after the hour it may not be in yet, and the box then shows the newest hour on record, marked as delayed.
Accuracy
The typical size of the this-hour model's miss over the last 7 days (mean absolute error, in MW, and as a percentage of demand).
Prediction vs actual graph
Orange is what the model predicted, dark is what really happened. Solid dots were predicted before the hour began; hollow dots were calculated afterwards from past data, so they are a little less honest as a test.

Using the explorer

  • Which factors move demand most? Each bar shows how far demand could swing as that one factor ranges over its whole range, with everything else held where the sliders are. Longer bar = bigger influence.
  • Prediction across each factor. One small graph per factor. The dot is where things are now; the curve shows what would happen elsewhere. Tick "same y-axis" to compare factors on equal terms, or untick it to see each curve's shape up close.
  • The sliders. Temperature change shifts the whole weather picture warmer or colder. The others set that factor directly. Press Reset to the forecast to go back to the real forecast for the hour you picked.

The six models in plain language

Every model comes in two flavours. "+ recent demand" models are shown how much electricity Ontario used in the last few hours (or, for the 7-day forecast, a week or two ago), which makes them very accurate for the next hour or two. ", no demand" models see just the weather and the calendar (hour, weekday, holidays, season) and never any demand figures, so they show how far weather alone can take you. There are three techniques:

Polynomial. One fixed mathematical formula that turns the inputs into a demand number. Everything it does is visible on its page, down to every coefficient.
Gradient boosting. A team of hundreds of small decision trees, each correcting the mistakes of the ones before it. Good at sharp effects such as holidays and the morning ramp.
Neural network. Layers of simple weighted sums with a bend between them, trained with PyTorch and averaged over several training runs. It can learn smooth, complicated patterns but, unlike the polynomial, can't be read as a single formula.

The site also shows the IESO's own forecast (the grid operator's official prediction) next to the models, as the benchmark to beat.

How accurate is it?

Expect the next-hour predictions to be off by roughly one to two percent, and the 7-day forecast to be somewhat worse because it depends on weather forecasts, which get less certain days ahead. The Comparison page has the exact scores. Odd days (holidays, storms, heat waves, outages) are the hardest to predict.

Where the numbers come from

  • Demand: the IESO's public reports (hourly history and 5-minute live readings).
  • Weather: Open-Meteo, averaged over seven Ontario cities weighted roughly by population.
  • The IESO's forecast: the grid operator's published hourly forecast, saved before each hour so it can be scored fairly.
  • Updates: an automatic job runs about every 10 minutes and publishes new numbers. These pages reload their data every 5 minutes on their own, so you can leave them open.

If something looks wrong

A yellow bar appears at the top
The data is out of date or one of the sources (weather, IESO feeds) is failing. The bar says which. The predictions shown are the last good ones.
A box shows "-" or the page says "No data yet"
The first automatic update hasn't run, or the data files haven't been published yet.
"Actual demand this hour" shows a dash early in the hour
The IESO's first readings for a new hour take a few minutes to arrive. The box only ever shows the current hour, never an older one.
The "this hour" prediction says it came from the 7-day forecast
The model needs the previous hour's demand, which isn't available yet. It falls back to the 7-day forecast run and says so; the proper number appears within a few minutes.
Times look an hour off
Everything is shown in Toronto time, including during daylight saving. The IESO itself reports in standard time all year, and the site converts for you.
